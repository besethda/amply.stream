Why it works this way

The awkward questions.

Amply makes some unusual choices. Here is the reasoning behind each one, including where the choice is a trade rather than a win.

What happens if someone doesn't pay?

If you set a price, your music is paid for. Someone who won't pay doesn't get to listen.

Here is exactly how that works.

No wallet, no paid music. Once you charge, every track you publish is paid for, except the ones you choose to make free. Your streaming service refuses to play a paid track to anyone whose app can't prove which wallet it holds. That is enforced by your server, not left to the listener's honesty: someone without a wallet hears your free tracks, and nothing else.

Listeners with a wallet pay as they listen. The Amply app pays for every minute of paid music automatically, without asking each time. Nobody has to remember to pay you.

Anyone who listens without paying loses your music. Your Earnings tab lists every wallet that has played your paid tracks, next to what that wallet has actually paid you, read straight off the public ledger — so nobody has to be taken at their word. Choose Stop serving on a wallet and your paid tracks stop playing for it within half an hour. Their app tells them why.

Free is your choice, track by track. Use Make free on any track and it plays for anyone, with or without a wallet — a single to share, a demo, a way in for someone who has never heard of you. Everything else is paid.

No money, no paid music either. An empty wallet is no better than none, so the Amply app won't play a paid track from a wallet that has nothing in it to pay with. A new wallet has to be funded before it hears anything you charge for.

Honest limits. Your server can check that a listener has a wallet, but not that they have paid — Solana blocks it from reading the ledger, so that check happens in your editor, in your browser, when you open it. Someone who builds their own app to skip paying can therefore listen until you notice and stop serving them. A barred listener can make a new wallet, and it appears in your Earnings like any other. And anything already downloaded stays downloaded.

Why crypto, and not PayPal or Stripe?

Because of what a four-cent payment costs to make. A card payment carries a fixed fee of around 20 to 30 cents plus a percentage, so a small payment is eaten entirely by the fee. Stripe and PayPal are built for buying things, not for paying a musician as their song plays. A transfer on Solana costs a fraction of a cent, which is what makes paying per minute possible at all.

There is a second reason, and it matters more. Card payments need an account holding money, which needs a company, which needs someone to trust and someone to take a cut. The moment Amply holds your money, Amply can lose it, freeze it, take a percentage of it, or be made to hand it over. A wallet-to-wallet transfer has no middle, which is why nobody takes 30% and why Amply can honestly say it takes nothing.

The trade, plainly: your listeners need a wallet with some USDC in it, and that is harder than typing a card number. So there is a card option too, for paying in one go rather than by the minute — see the next question.

Can listeners subscribe by card instead?

Yes, if you want them to. Under Subscriptions in your editor you connect your own Stripe account and set a price for a year, six months and three months (we suggest $10, $6 and $3). A subscriber hears all your paid music for that time and is never charged by the minute. Card fees are fine here because the payment is a few dollars, not a few cents.

The step-by-step guide to connecting Stripe takes about fifteen minutes, and you can try it all with pretend money first.

You choose. Per-minute only, subscriptions only, or both — in which case each listener picks. Subscriptions never replace the per-minute price; turning them on just adds a second way in.

It's your Stripe account, not ours. Stripe pays you directly, takes its usual fee (around 3% plus 30¢ in the US), and Amply takes nothing and never sees the money. Your Stripe key is stored on your streaming service, used only to set up your plans and check who has subscribed, and never shown again — not to you, and not to Amply. You can use a restricted key, which can do only those things.

How your streaming service knows who subscribed. The listener's app sends its wallet's address to Stripe with the payment. Afterwards, your streaming service asks Stripe about that payment, and checks each subscription again when it's due to renew, and once a day, so renewals and cancellations are noticed. The wallet is only used as a name here: a subscriber doesn't need any money in it. That's also why they should keep its backup key — it's what carries the subscription to a new phone.

Cancelling and refunds happen on Stripe's own page, which listeners open from your page in the app. Refunds are up to you, in your Stripe dashboard. Selling subscriptions makes you a seller in the ordinary sense: in the EU that usually means a 14-day right to cancel and VAT on what you sell, which Stripe Tax can work out for you. We can't give you tax or legal advice.

Which wallet should I use?

Phantom. It's free, it works on your phone or in your browser, and setting it up takes a couple of minutes. Copy the address it gives you into Who gets paid in your editor, and you're done. You don't need to put anything in it to start receiving.

Any Solana wallet works — Solflare and others are fine — and your listeners don't need to use the same one as you. Money goes from any Solana wallet to any Solana address.

The one rule that matters: USDC on Solana. USDC is the digital dollar Amply uses, and it exists on several different blockchains that can't reach each other. USDC sent over Ethereum, Base or any other network will not arrive at a Solana address, and usually can't be recovered. Whenever an app or an exchange asks which network, the answer is Solana.

Don't use an exchange's deposit address as your payment address. Coinbase, Kraken and others will give you an address, but they are built for occasional large deposits, not a steady trickle of small ones, and many have a minimum below which a deposit isn't credited. Receive into your own wallet, and move money to an exchange when you want to cash out.

Getting it into your bank. Send USDC from your wallet to an exchange that operates where you live — choosing Solana as the network — sell it for your own currency, and withdraw. Doing that occasionally, in larger amounts, keeps the fees down.

Your recovery phrase is the money. When you set up your wallet it will show you a list of words. Anyone who has them can take everything in it, and if you lose them and your phone, it's gone. Write them down on paper and keep them somewhere safe. Amply can't recover them. Nobody can.

Why do payments arrive in batches?

Because a transfer costs a fraction of a cent even when the amount is tiny, and one per song would spend more on postage than it needs to.

Your listener is not approving these. The app carries its own small wallet, kept on their phone, so it pays as they listen: no popup, no confirmation, nothing to tap. They agree your price once, the first time they play you, and after that it simply happens. Batching is about the cost of a transfer, not about how often they are interrupted.

The app keeps a running total of what it owes you and sends it when it reaches the amount you chose: every song, every five, or every ten. Nobody is holding that money in the meantime, and there is no account it sits in. The tally lives on the listener's own device, and the payment goes straight from their wallet to yours.

Anything below your amount is sent within seven days at the latest, so somebody who plays four songs and never comes back still pays for four songs — the next time they open the app. It isn't sent the moment they pause: that would mean payments of a cent, when you asked for ten. Your editor only marks a listener as behind once they owe well past your amount.

Choosing "every ten songs" does not mean waiting for ten songs. It means at most ten.

How should I choose a price?

For reference, a stream on Spotify pays an artist somewhere around a third of a cent, before anyone else takes their share. A cent a minute on Amply is about four cents for a four-minute song, and all of it is yours.

So the honest framing is not "how do I match Spotify" but "what would a listener who actually likes me be glad to pay". Most artists should start at a cent a minute and ignore the slider afterwards.

Why does a listener need a wallet?

Because it is how your streaming service knows who is listening, so it can refuse the people who don't pay.

The wallet does two jobs at once: it pays you, and it identifies the listener. There is no account, no password and no email. The listener's app proves which wallet it holds by signing a one-off challenge from your server, and your server issues it a pass for half an hour.

What it costs you: a record of each wallet that plays your paid music becomes your responsibility under data protection law. Your streaming service keeps totals only — how much, not what or when — which keeps that responsibility as small as it can be, and any listener can erase their own record without asking you.

What does data protection ask of me?

Less than you'd fear, and most of it is already done. But you should know what it is, because it's yours.

If you charge, your streaming service keeps a small record for each wallet that plays your paid music. Under GDPR that makes you the record's controller: the person responsible for it. Amply isn't — it holds nothing and can't see your server.

What you hold: a wallet address, how many tracks and minutes it has played, when it first and last listened, and whether you've stopped serving it. What you don't: which tracks anyone played, or when, or any name, email or account. Nothing at all for tracks you've made free.

Already done for you:

  • A privacy notice. Your streaming service writes one at your-address/privacy, from what it actually keeps, so it can't be wrong or out of date. Your page links to it.
  • Seeing and erasing. A listener can see what you hold about them, download it, or erase it, straight from the Amply app. Their app proves the record is theirs by signing for it. You don't have to do anything.
  • Not keeping it forever. A record is deleted automatically a year after that wallet was last heard from.
  • Your hosting. Cloudflare runs your server in your own account, and its data processing terms are part of the agreement you accepted when you opened it.

Still yours to do:

  • Add a contact address in Settings. A controller has to be reachable, and it goes on your privacy page.
  • Answer anyone who writes to you about their data, within a month. Your Earnings tab has Export and Delete on every listener, which is everything a request needs.
  • Look after your Cloudflare account — a strong password and two-step sign-in. If it were broken into in a way likely to put listeners at risk, you might have to tell your data protection authority within 72 hours.

This is how the software is built, not legal advice. If your circumstances are unusual — a label, staff, listeners in the thousands — it's worth an hour with someone who does this for a living.

How do you know who has paid me?

We don't. Your editor works it out, in your browser, while you are looking at it.

Payments to you are public: they are transfers on a public ledger, and anyone can read them, including you. Your editor reads every payment ever sent to the addresses in your Settings, groups them by the wallet that sent them, and puts that column next to the listening your streaming service recorded. The two line up because they are keyed by the same thing: the wallet is the identity, so nobody has to report anything for the match to work.

Nothing is stored as a result. Your streaming service keeps no ledger of who paid, because the chain is already that ledger and it cannot be lost or tampered with. Close the editor and the figures are simply read again next time.

Two honest limits. The "paid" figure is everything that wallet has ever sent you, while "listened" counts only your paid tracks, so the comparison is rough — and if you raised your price, old listening looks underpaid when it was not. Treat a red figure as something to look at, not a verdict.

What happens when I stop serving someone?

That wallet stops being able to play your paid tracks, within about half an hour. Your free tracks keep playing for them, because free means free.

They can make a new wallet in under a minute. The Amply app won't play your paid music from it until it has money in it, and it shows up in your Earnings like any other. And anything already downloaded stays downloaded: you cannot un-hear music.

What it does survive is being asked to be forgotten. A listener can erase their record from your streaming service at any time, and if you have barred them, the erasure takes every number with it and leaves the bar standing. Your decision is not undone by their right.

There is no shared blocklist. The list lives on your own streaming service, nobody else can see it, and Amply holds nothing.

Where does the money actually go?

From the listener's wallet to the address in your manifest, in one transaction, with nothing in between. Amply is not a recipient and not a router; there is no account for it to rest in, which is the same reason we cannot take a cut even if we wanted to.

If you have named collaborators, the split happens inside that single transaction, so either everybody is paid or nobody is. It cannot half-happen.

Do I have to pay tax on this?

We cannot advise you, and would be wrong to try. What we can tell you is roughly where the lines are, so nothing arrives as a surprise.

In Sweden you do not have to register for VAT until your turnover passes 120,000 kr a year. Selling digital things to buyers in other EU countries has its own threshold of €10,000 a year, below which you charge your own country's rate. Both of those arrive at a point where you can afford an accountant, which is the right moment to get one.

Money reaching your wallet is income wherever you live. Amply sends no forms and files nothing on your behalf, because Amply is not part of the transaction.

Why do I have to rebuild my streaming service?

Because it runs in your Cloudflare account, and we have no way in. That is the point of the design: nobody at Amply holds a key to your music.

The cost is that improvements do not arrive by themselves. Your editor notices when there is a newer version and offers a rebuild, which takes about a minute and does not touch your music, your page or your price.
